> **In one sentence.** A base model knows the language; post-training teaches it to hold up its end of a conversation, to prefer answers people prefer, and lately to think before it speaks.

Every model you talk to has been through two lives. The first, pretraining,
made a text predictor. The second, **post-training**, shaped that predictor
into the thing with a name and a personality that ships as `-Instruct`.
The second life is far cheaper than the first, a few percent of the
compute, and it is where most of the differences between models of the
same size come from. It is also the part you can most plausibly do
yourself, which is why the next page is about your workbench.

## Supervised fine-tuning

The first step is the simplest: show the model conversations. A
**supervised fine-tuning** (SFT) set is tens of thousands to a few million
examples of a user turn and the assistant turn that should follow, laid
out with the model's **chat template**, and the model is trained with
the same next-token loss as pretraining, usually only on the assistant's
tokens. The result answers questions instead of continuing them, follows
the format it was shown, and picks up the tone of its examples.

Where the examples come from is most of the craft. Early sets were
written by contractors; today most are generated by a stronger model,
filtered, and sometimes rewritten (this is **distillation**, below), with
human-written data reserved for the behaviours that matter most.

## Preference optimization

SFT teaches *a* good answer. It cannot teach *better*, because it never
sees two answers side by side. **Preference optimization** does: pairs of
responses to the same prompt, one chosen and one rejected, from humans
or from a model acting as judge.

- **RLHF** with **PPO** (2022) trained a separate **reward model** on the pairs, then ran reinforcement learning against it: sample an answer, score it, nudge the policy toward higher scores, with a penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model. Expensive and fiddly, and it made ChatGPT.
- **DPO** (2023) showed the same objective can be optimized directly on the pairs with a single loss and no reward model or sampling loop. Most open post-training since has used DPO or a cousin (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO).
- **GRPO** (2024, DeepSeek) returned to sampling, several answers per prompt, scored and normalized against each other, with no separate value model. It is cheap enough to run at scale and is the workhorse of the reasoning era.

The preferences encode what the publisher wanted: helpfulness, safety
policy, verbosity, a house style. Two models with the same base and
different preference data are different assistants.

## Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards

The 2025 turn was to stop asking a judge and ask a **checker**. For math
with a known answer, code with unit tests, or a puzzle with a rule, the
reward is exact: one if the final answer is right, zero if not. Run GRPO
on tens of thousands of such problems, let the model generate as long as
it likes before its final answer, and it learns to *use* the length:
to plan, try, check, and backtrack in text. That text is the **reasoning
trace**, the "thinking" between `<think>` tags, and models trained this
way (DeepSeek-R1, the o-series, Qwen3's thinking mode, GLM's) are the
**reasoning models**. Nothing about the architecture changed; the
post-training did.

A **hybrid** model (`Qwen3`, `GLM-5.3`) ships one set of weights with a
switch: a template flag turns thinking on or off. A **-Thinking** suffix
on a Hub name means the trace is always on.

## Distillation

**Distillation** trains a smaller model to imitate a larger one. In the
simplest and most common form, the teacher answers a large set of prompts
and the student is SFT-trained on the answers, so a `DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B`
is a Qwen 7B base fine-tuned on 800,000 R1 reasoning traces. In the
stricter form the student matches the teacher's whole next-token
distribution, not just its chosen tokens, which needs the teacher's
logits at training time. Either way the student's behaviour comes from
the teacher, and its lineage has two parents: the base it started as, and
the model it learned from. A model card's `base_model` tags can name both.

## Merges, and edits without data

Two fine-tunes of the same base can be **merged** by averaging their
weights, or by adding their differences from the base (**task vectors**),
often with tricks to keep the additions from cancelling (TIES, DARE,
SLERP). It works better than it should, because fine-tunes of a common
base live near each other in weight space. Merges are cheap, need no
data, and are a large share of the community models on the Hub. Their
lineage is a list of parents and a recipe file.

**Abliteration** is an edit with no training at all: find the direction
in activation space that the model uses to refuse, and project it out of
the weights. The result complies with requests the original declined, at
some cost to everything else. It is a clear example of a derived artifact
that is *not* a print of its parent: the direction was computed from
specific prompts on a specific machine, and the exact bytes depend on
both. The **abliterated** lens on a board reads this from the name.

## The chat template

All of the above assumes a **template**: the exact text that wraps a
conversation so the model sees `<|im_start|>user … <|im_end|>` where it
expects to. It lives in `tokenizer_config.json` or a `chat_template.jinja`
file and is part of the post-trained artifact. Serve a model with the
wrong template and it still speaks, badly: it may answer for the user,
never stop, or ignore the system prompt. Runtimes read the template from
the files; that is one of the reasons darsay keeps them.

## What ships

A post-trained release is the same shape as a base release, the same
config, the same tokenizer, the same size, plus the template and a
`generation_config.json` with the sampling settings the publisher tuned
for. What is usually *not* released is the data that made it, which is
why the weights are the artifact here too.

<div class="docs-note"><strong>Reading a name.</strong> <code>-Base</code> is the pretrained predictor. <code>-Instruct</code> or <code>-Chat</code> is SFT plus preference-tuned. <code>-Thinking</code> reasons before every answer. <code>-Distill-</code> learned from a named teacher. <code>-abliterated</code> or <code>-uncensored</code> was edited after the fact. None of these are guarantees; they are what the publisher chose to say. The <a href="/docs/learn/lineage/">lineage page</a> is about how far to trust them.</div>

## Where this bites

- **Base and Instruct are different artifacts.** Same size, same architecture, different behaviour. Keep the one you will use; keep both if you intend to post-train.
- **A reasoning model spends tokens.** Ten to fifty times more output per answer. Budget context and time for the trace.
- **Distills are not the teacher.** A 7B distilled from a 671B reasons in the teacher's style at the student's capacity.
- **Merges can be brittle.** They usually work; when they do not, the failure is in the parts of the two parents that disagreed. Test before trusting.
